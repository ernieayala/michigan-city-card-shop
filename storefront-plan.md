# Storefront Plan

> Last updated: 2026-10-03
> Status: draft. Built from the six research files and the owner's operating
> choices. No quote, count or sales figure in it has been collected yet; the
> checks in section 2 exist to collect them.

## Summary

A card store on Franklin Street, run by two co-owners, the owner full time and
a partner on event nights, with no employees. Sales are 80% trading card games
and 20% sports cards, with events most nights, and the store sells online on
TCGplayer, eBay and Whatnot as well as over the counter. A Wyoming parent LLC
owns the Indiana LLC that runs the store. It starts small and adds money in
stages. Stage 1, buying and selling at card shows and online with no lease,
costs $11,775 to $21,775. Stage 2, an 858 sq ft store with 20 seats, costs
$40,470 to $44,874 before credit for cards carried in from stage 1. Both stages
together come to about $47,200 to $61,200. The store breaks even at about
$6,800 a month in sales. Splitting $3,000 a month of profit takes about $19,800
a month and another $22,719 of stock, added in stage 3; splitting $6,000 takes
about $32,800, more than the area's estimated card spending, so that level
depends on online sales. Nine checks (gates, section 2) must pass before any
lease.

Every figure carries a confidence tier as defined on the [overview](README.md).
Figures marked "plan" are choices this plan makes; figures marked "owner" are
the owner's own observations or decisions.

### Stage 1: shows and online

Buy collections at card shows, sell high-value cards at shows and lower-value
cards online, and log every buy and sale. Sports cards are part of it from the
first show: the partner buys and sells sports singles and graded cards. This
stage measures what the rest of the plan guesses: the buy rates that win
collections, the value mix of what people sell, and what local buyers pay. It
also builds the TCGplayer seller level the store needs: new sellers start at
100 items and $1,000 listed and reach the top level after 51 orders
([online and legal](research/online-and-legal.md), section 1.2, fact).

**Before stage 1 starts:** the Planning department confirms in writing that
shipping orders from home is allowed (or another shipping address is arranged),
and the state and county rules for buying used goods from the public are known.
If TCGplayer registration is still paused, lower-value cards sell on eBay or
Whatnot, or at shows.

### Ruled out by the research

As a lasting business, online-only is ruled out: distributors refuse
online-only sellers, and cards bought at 80% lose money on TCGplayer. The store
sells online only the lower-value cards bought well below that. A counter
inside another business is also ruled out: Southern Hobby refuses stores
operating within another business, and WPN requires a store that does not
share space ([distribution and supply](research/distribution-and-supply.md),
sections 1.2 and 2.1).

## Headline, pessimistic first

1. **The store breaks even at $6,786 a month in sales; splitting $3,000 a month
   of profit takes $19,811 and splitting $6,000 takes $32,836** (section 5,
   estimate built on guesses). In-store sales at $32,836 a month would be 110%
   of the trade area's estimated card spend at the 2025 peak (guess). Online
   sales and customers from beyond Michigan City carry the upper levels.
2. **Buy rates set the margin.** Paying 80% of market on every card and selling
   at market leaves 20% on singles. This plan pays 80% only on cards worth $50
   or more and less on cheaper cards, which blends to 63.75% of market value
   and a 36.3% singles margin in the store (section 1, guess). If collections
   come in mostly high-value, the margin moves toward 20%.
3. **Online sales reach more buyers at a lower margin.** Cards bought at 65% or
   less earn about 16.6% online after fees and postage, against 36.3% over the
   counter; cards bought at 80% lose money online (section 1, estimate built on
   guesses). Doubling the online share raises break-even from $6,786 to $7,593
   but cuts how much of the local market the store needs.
4. **Opening the store takes $40,470 to $44,874** (section 4, estimate built on
   guesses), less credit for cards carried in from stage 1. That stocks the
   store for break-even; each $3,000 a month of profit to split needs about
   $22,719 more stock.
5. **Sports cards have no direct sealed supply for a new store.** Topps is
   closed to new shops and Panini requires 70% of sales in sports. The store
   buys sports sealed product from online retailers at secondary prices, about
   10% margin, and makes its sports money on singles and graded cards
   (section 7).

Yearly upkeep: $60 + $100 + $32 / 2 = $176, about $15 a month, in the section 5

What the structure does and does not change (general knowledge, not checked
against a source; gate G9 confirms each point):

- The Indiana LLC collects Indiana sales tax and its income is taxed as
  Indiana business income; the Wyoming parent does not change that.
- With two co-owners as members, the LLC that has both as members files a
  partnership tax return, and each owner pays self-employment tax on their
  share. Members who work in their own LLC are not employees, so the store
  carries no payroll and no workers compensation.
- A personal guarantee on the lease or on distributor credit reaches the
  owners personally, whatever the structure.
- If the Wyoming LLC itself does business in Indiana, it registers there as a
  foreign LLC: $125 (Indiana Foreign Registration Statement, State Form 56369,
  revised 01-26, fact).

## 6. Stock and cash

A store that sells through its stock five times a year (a plan assumption), at
a 72.7% cost ratio (1 - 27.3%), needs 6,786 x 12 x 0.72678 / 5 = $11,837
of stock at cost for $6,786 a month in sales: the opening stock. One store
owner's rule in the research says a margin under 40% needs about ten turns a
year, so five is optimistic. Each $3,000 a month of profit to split adds
$13,025 a month of sales and $22,719 of stock.

The operating cash covers three to six months of costs with zero sales. The
build-out months pay rent, utilities, internet and alarm from the build-out
occupancy line in section 4; the store's other monthly costs start at opening,
with the operating cash. The rent-free build-out credit counts two months of
rent; if the landlord waives all three build-out months, the saving is three
months.
